In one line: LecGap watches a lecture, figures out which concepts you need to understand before others, and uses that to fix exactly what you're confused about — while also showing the professor where the whole class got stuck. 1. The Problem — In Plain English

Recorded lectures are everywhere now — NPTEL, Coursera, YouTube EDU channels, and every college that started recording classes during and after COVID. This isn't a niche situation anymore; it's how a huge number of students actually learn a huge portion of their coursework. And yet the experience of watching one hasn't improved much in a decade — it's still just a long video with a scrub bar.

Imagine you're watching a 45-minute recorded lecture on neural networks. Halfway through, the professor explains "backpropagation" and you're completely lost. What do you do? You could rewatch the whole video, but that wastes time. You could Google it, but now you're reading a totally different explanation that might not match how your professor teaches it, using different notation and different examples.

Here's the real problem: you probably didn't get confused at "backpropagation" itself — you got confused because you never fully understood activation functions, which backpropagation depends on. But nothing tells you that. You just know you're lost, not why you're lost or what to fix first. So you either give up, or you brute-force rewatch everything, hoping you stumble back onto the part that actually matters.

Think of it like... A textbook has an index, a table of contents, and chapter dependencies clearly implied by its structure — "Chapter 4 assumes you've read Chapter 2." A video lecture has none of that. It's just one long stream you have to sit through, with no map of what depends on what.

This compounds over an entire course. If you're a little shaky on Week 3's material, and Week 3 is secretly a prerequisite for Week 4, 5, and 6, that one small gap quietly grows into a much bigger one by the time you reach the exam — and by then it's genuinely hard to tell which of the ten things you don't understand is the actual root cause.

And it's not just students who are stuck. The professor has no idea that 70% of the class silently got lost at the exact same point in the video — they only find out during exams, when it's too late to help, and when there's no way to know if the confusion was about that specific concept or something taught two weeks earlier that it depended on.

2. What Already Exists (and Why It's Not Enough)

Tools like YouLearn, Knowt, and NotebookLM

These are real, popular apps (YouLearn alone reports millions of users) that let you paste in a lecture video and get back a quiz, flashcards, and a summary of what you got wrong. Genuinely useful — but they treat every question you got wrong as a separate, unconnected fact. They don't know that missing question 3 is because you never understood the idea from question 1. NotebookLM goes a step further with a "Mind Map" feature that visualizes how topics relate — but it's a generic, undirected web of connections, not a system that decides "A must come before B" and can be checked for being right or wrong.

YouTube's own chapters and captions

YouTube can split a video into chapters and let you search the spoken transcript. But chapters are the same for every single viewer — they don't adapt to what you specifically don't understand. And if a professor draws a diagram on a whiteboard without narrating it, YouTube's caption search has nothing to find, because it only "hears" a lecture, it never "sees" it.

Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) — real research, not a lecture tool

This is an established academic technique that estimates the probability a specific student "knows" a given concept, based on their past quiz answers. It's genuinely useful and we take inspiration from it — but it predicts an individual's mastery, it doesn't build or maintain a map of which concepts depend on which, and it was never designed to work directly on raw lecture video.

Adaptive learning platforms — Khan Academy, ALEKS

These do have real prerequisite logic — ALEKS in particular is well known for adapting to what a student does and doesn't know. But that logic is built by hand, once, into a fixed, hand-authored course structure that the platform's own creators wrote. You can't point either tool at an arbitrary lecture video from your own professor and have it work — the prerequisite map only exists because humans built the entire curriculum around it in advance.

"Couldn't a professor just tag the prerequisites by hand?"

Technically yes, and it's actually what the very first version of this project's plan proposed. But it doesn't scale: someone has to sit down and manually decide every single "concept A needs concept B" relationship, for every course, every semester, and redo it whenever the syllabus changes even slightly. And a hand-written list can never improve itself — it's frozen the moment it's written, no matter how students actually use it.

Why LecGap is different — #1 Every tool above either treats your wrong answers as a flat, unconnected list, or requires a human to build the prerequisite structure by hand in advance. LecGap is the only one that learns which concepts depend on which automatically, directly from raw lecture content — and then checks its own accuracy against a real, human-labeled answer key, rather than assuming it got it right.

3. The Big Idea

The core idea behind LecGap is simple to say, even though it takes real engineering to build: automatically figure out which concepts in a course require which other concepts, use that map to fix learning gaps in the right order, and keep improving that map using real data — instead of freezing it the moment it's built.

Think of it like... A video game's skill tree. You can't unlock the "advanced fire spell" until you've unlocked "basic fire spell." LecGap builds that same kind of tree, but for the concepts in your course — automatically, by watching the lecture, instead of a human manually deciding the tree by hand.

There are really three separate insights stacked on top of each other here, and it's worth pulling them apart because each one answers a different objection:

  1. Learn it automatically, not by hand. This is what makes it scale to any course, not just ones someone has already curated.
  2. Prove it's right, don't just assume it. This is what separates LecGap from a system that just "sounds smart" — every claim about accuracy is checked against real, independently-labeled data.
  3. Let it keep learning, don't freeze it. This is what turns a one-time script into a system that actually gets better the longer it's used — the same way a spam filter improves the more real email it sees.

Almost every existing tool does at most one of these three things. LecGap does all three, in one working pipeline.

4. How It Actually Works — Step by Step

Here's the full journey, from a raw lecture video to a smarter classroom. Every technical term is explained the moment it shows up, and a couple of steps include a worked example so the idea has something concrete to hang onto.

1 Turning speech into text

First, the system needs to know what was actually said. We use Whisper, a speech-to-text AI (built by OpenAI) that listens to the lecture audio and writes it out as text, with a timestamp on every sentence — so we always know when something was said, down to the second. This timestamped transcript is the raw material every later step is built on.

2 Figuring out what's actually being taught

Now we have a transcript, but a transcript isn't the same as a list of concepts. We use an LLM (Large Language Model — the same kind of AI behind ChatGPT or Claude) to read the transcript and pull out the actual ideas being taught. It can even catch concepts the professor never says by name — like recognizing a lecture is really about the "bias-variance tradeoff" even if that exact phrase is never spoken aloud.

But some things are only ever shown, never said — a diagram, an equation on a whiteboard, a bullet point on a slide. For that, we use CLIP (an AI that matches images to text descriptions, without needing to be specially trained for lectures) and OCR ("Optical Character Recognition" — software that reads text out of an image, like scanning a photo of a whiteboard). To keep this efficient, we don't process every single video frame — we only sample frames at scene changes (e.g. when the camera cuts or the slide changes), which is enough to catch new visual content without wasting compute on near-identical frames.

Why LecGap is different — #2 Every competitor listed above only processes audio. If a professor silently writes an equation on the board, those tools never even know it happened. LecGap "watches" the video too, not just "listens" to it.

3 Figuring out what needs to be learned first

This is the heart of the whole project. For every pair of concepts, the system has to decide: does understanding concept A actually require understanding concept B first? We do this with a model that's been through fine-tuning — meaning we take an AI that already has a broad, general understanding of language (rather than starting from nothing) and give it focused extra training specifically on identifying prerequisite relationships.

Think of it like... Hiring a chef who already knows how to cook in general, then giving them a short course on your restaurant's specific menu — instead of teaching someone to cook from absolute zero. It's faster and far more reliable, especially since we only have a modest amount of labeled example data (a few hundred examples) to learn from.

Behind the scenes, concepts are represented as embeddings — a way of turning ideas into points on an invisible map, so that similar ideas sit close together and the computer can measure "how related" two concepts are, rather than just checking if they share the same words.

One practical detail worth knowing: we don't check every possible pair of concepts against every other. A single course might have 100+ concepts, which means tens of thousands of possible pairs — far too many to check exhaustively. Instead, we only check pairs that are likely candidates in the first place (concepts that appear close together in time, or that are semantically similar), and an LLM reasoning pass double-checks the classifier's guess and explains why it thinks one concept precedes another — that explanation later shows up in the instructor dashboard.

4 Drawing the map

Once we know which concepts depend on which, we connect them into a directed graph — think of it as a one-way flowchart, with arrows pointing from "must-learn-first" concepts toward the ones that depend on them, and never looping back on itself. We build this using a tool called NetworkX.

As new lectures get added, newly found concepts are checked against what's already in the graph using embedding similarity, so "Gradient Descent" from one lecture and "GD optimization" from a different lecture get merged into a single node instead of creating a confusing duplicate.

Why this doesn't spiral out of control The graph only ever contains the concepts from one course — realistically 50 to 150 concepts — not an attempt to map all of human knowledge. Building one giant graph connecting every concept in every field is a genuinely different, much harder research problem (the kind of effort projects like Wikidata have spent years on), and it's simply not what LecGap is trying to do.

5 Cutting the video into bite-sized pieces

Using the timestamps from Step 1, we use a tool called FFmpeg to physically cut the lecture video into short clips — one per concept — so a student never has to scrub through a 45-minute video to find the 90 seconds that actually matter to them.

6 The quiz and the personalized fix

A student takes a short quiz. If they get a question wrong, the system doesn't just say "you got this wrong" — it checks the graph from Step 4 and shows them the clips in the correct learning order, starting from the true root cause, not just the symptom.

Why LecGap is different — #3 This is the exact feature nothing else on the market has: turning "here's what you got wrong" into "here's what to relearn, and in what order, based on an actual model of how concepts depend on each other."

7 Getting smarter over time

Here's the part that makes LecGap a living system instead of a one-time script: if lots of students keep failing concept B right after struggling with concept A, that real pattern gets fed back in to correct the graph — strengthening a connection the system got right, or fixing one it got wrong. This is called a refinement loop, and it uses a technique called weak supervision — using indirect, imperfect real-world signals (like quiz patterns) to improve a model, instead of needing a human to hand-label everything perfectly.

Being honest about this part We don't have access to hundreds of real students yet, so we test this loop using synthetic students — an AI is prompted to "role-play" as a student who has only learned a specific, randomized set of concepts, and it genuinely reasons through quiz questions the way that student would. This proves the mechanism works — it does not yet prove real human students behave exactly like the simulation. We say this plainly rather than hiding it.
A worked example Say the real (hidden) order is: Loss Functions → Gradient Descent → Backpropagation → Neural Networks. We keep this hidden from the system — it's our private answer key. We then simulate 500 "students," each knowing a random subset of these four concepts. A simulated student who doesn't know Gradient Descent will realistically also struggle with Backpropagation, because our LLM-persona student reasons through the question honestly rather than just rolling dice. We feed all of these simulated quiz results into the system's own guessed graph (which starts out imperfect, like a real model would). If, after processing this data, the system's guess lines up more closely with the hidden four-step order than it did before, that's real, measurable evidence the refinement mechanism works.

8 Helping the professor

Every student's quiz results get combined into a dashboard for the instructor — a confusion heatmap (think of a weather map, but instead of temperature, it shows where in the lecture the most students got confused). It also shows something no other tool does: where the order the professor taught things in doesn't match the order the data says students actually needed to learn them in.

Why this matters concretely Imagine a professor teaches Concept Y in week 3 and Concept X (which the data shows Y actually depends on) in week 5. Nobody would notice this just from watching the class — but LecGap's dashboard would flag it directly, giving the instructor a concrete, evidence-backed reason to consider reordering their syllabus next semester.
Why LecGap is different — #4 Every AI study tool we compared against is built only for individual students — there's no concept of a "classroom" at all. LecGap is the only one that closes the loop back to the person teaching the class, not just the people learning from it.

5. Proving It Actually Works (Not Just Claiming It)

Anyone can claim their AI "understands" prerequisites. The difference between a claim and a fact is evaluation — so here's exactly how we check ourselves, in plain terms.

The benchmark: LectureBank

LectureBank is a public dataset built by researchers (not us) — 1,352 real lecture files from 60 real courses, with 208 concept pairs that real people manually checked and labeled as "yes, this is a genuine prerequisite" or "no, it isn't," spanning five subjects (NLP, Machine Learning, AI, Deep Learning, and Information Retrieval). It's an answer key we didn't write ourselves — so when we test our classifier against it, the score is real, not something we can quietly fudge.

There's actually a larger, newer version called "LectureBank2.0," but we deliberately use the original 1.0 version instead — 2.0 is described by its own creators as drawn largely from NLP specifically, while 1.0's spread across five different subjects is a much closer match to the kind of ML/DL lectures we're actually testing on.

What "accuracy" actually means here

We measure two things, both standard in machine learning: precision ("of all the prerequisite connections we claimed were real, how many actually were, according to the answer key?") and recall ("of all the real prerequisite connections in the answer key, how many did we actually catch?"). We also use cross-validation — splitting the labeled data into several chunks, testing on each chunk in turn while training on the rest — so our score reflects real performance, not a lucky one-time split of the data.

The recovery test for the refinement loop

As described in Step 7 above: hide a known-correct concept map, simulate realistic student behavior against it, and measure whether our system's guess gets more accurate over time. This is a standard, legitimate technique used in real machine learning research when live data isn't available yet — not something we invented to dodge real testing.

7. Quick Glossary — Every Term In One Place

Whisper
A speech-to-text AI made by OpenAI. It listens to audio and converts it into written text with timestamps.
LLM (Large Language Model)
The type of AI behind tools like ChatGPT and Claude — trained on huge amounts of text so it can read, summarize, and reason about language.
CLIP
An AI model that can match images to text descriptions without needing extra training for a specific task — used here to understand what's shown visually in a lecture.
OCR (Optical Character Recognition)
Software that reads and extracts text from an image — like turning a photo of a whiteboard into actual, searchable text.
Fine-tuning
Taking an AI model that's already broadly trained and giving it focused extra training on a specific task, instead of training a brand-new model completely from zero.
Embeddings
A way of representing words or ideas as points on an invisible map, so ideas that mean similar things end up located close together — letting a computer measure "how related" two things are.
Directed graph / DAG
A diagram made of boxes connected by one-way arrows, with no loops ("Directed Acyclic Graph") — used here to represent "this concept must be understood before that one."
Prerequisite graph
The specific directed graph LecGap builds for a course, mapping out which concepts depend on which others.
NetworkX
A Python software library used for building, storing, and analyzing graphs like the prerequisite graph — handles things like detecting cycles and ordering nodes correctly.
FFmpeg
A widely-used tool for editing and processing video/audio files — here, used to cut a lecture into short, concept-specific clips.
Refinement loop
The part of LecGap that watches real quiz results over time and uses them to correct and improve its concept graph.
Weak supervision
A machine learning technique that improves a model using indirect, imperfect real-world signals (like patterns in quiz answers) instead of needing every example to be perfectly hand-labeled by a person.
Synthetic students
AI-simulated "students," each given a randomized set of concepts they supposedly know, used to test whether LecGap's refinement loop actually works before testing it on real people.
Benchmark
A public dataset with known, correct answers, used to test how accurate a system really is — instead of just trusting a system's own claims.
LectureBank
The specific benchmark LecGap is evaluated against — 1,352 real lecture files and 208 human-labeled prerequisite concept pairs across five subjects.
Precision & recall
Two ways of scoring accuracy. Precision: of everything we claimed was true, how much actually was? Recall: of everything that was actually true, how much did we catch?
Cross-validation
Splitting a dataset into several chunks and testing a model on each chunk in turn (while training on the rest), so the accuracy score reflects real performance rather than one lucky split of the data.
Confusion heatmap
A visual, color-coded timeline showing exactly where in a lecture the most students got confused — similar to a weather map, but for confusion instead of temperature.
DKT (Deep Knowledge Tracing)
An established academic technique that predicts the probability a specific student currently understands a given concept, based on their history of past answers.
Decoupled architecture
Building the "brain" of the system (the backend, which does the actual processing) separately from the "face" of the system (the frontend, which is what the user sees and clicks on) — so either half can be changed without breaking the other.

8. What We're NOT Claiming

Being able to teach this project well also means being honest about its edges — a confident presenter who knows the limits sounds far more credible than one who claims perfection.

Stated plainly We are not claiming to have invented prerequisite-relation learning — it's existing academic research, active since at least 2015. We are not claiming the refinement loop has been proven on real classrooms yet — only in a controlled simulation. We are not claiming perfect accuracy outside the subjects LectureBank covers. What we are claiming is the specific combination — automatic extraction, evaluated prerequisites, personalized remediation, self-correction, and instructor analytics, all in one working system — which nothing else currently does.
